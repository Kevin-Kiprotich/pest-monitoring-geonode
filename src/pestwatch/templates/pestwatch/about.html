How PestWatch works

PestWatch maps where conditions favour crop pests and diseases in each county: fall armyworm, aphids, thrips, stem borer, African armyworm and maize lethal necrosis disease (MLND). The maps are produced automatically by models that combine weather, satellite and terrain data with field records of where each pest has been found.

The two maps

Every pest, county and date has two versions of the same result:

Hybrid risk (0 to 1)

A continuous score for every 30 m cell. 0 means conditions are among the least favourable in the county, 1 among the most favourable. It blends two models: 80 % a statistical model learned from field records, 20 % a temperature model built from the pest's known development thresholds.

0 · low0.51 · high

Risk class (1 to 5)

The same score grouped into five classes, easier to read and to summarise by ward. The class limits are set from the scores at known infestations, so "Very high" means conditions like those at most recorded outbreaks.

How a map is made

  1. Collect the weather. Every day the system downloads the latest observed rainfall (CHIRPS), temperature and wind (ERA5-Land) and humidity (ERA5) for each county. Every month it downloads the seasonal forecast.
  2. Prepare the forecast weather (forecast only). The forecast is corrected for its known errors by comparing past forecasts with what was observed, then sharpened to 30 m using elevation: higher ground is cooler.
  3. Forecast the vegetation (forecast only). The LSTM model predicts 18 satellite vegetation indices and bands for each of the coming months, cell by cell.
  4. Run the pest models. For each pest, an ensemble of six statistical models (MaxEnt, GAM, random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, SVM) scores every cell from 35-40 predictors: terrain, soil, crop type, weather over the last 7-30 days and the satellite indices. A temperature model adds what is known about the pest's biology. Aphids also use the fall armyworm map, and MLND uses the maps of its insect vectors.
  5. Publish. The hybrid score and its five classes are published as map layers, with summaries per county, constituency and ward.

Reading the maps

  • Risk is relative. A high score means conditions in that cell resemble those where the pest has been found, compared with the rest of the county. It is not a count of pests or a probability of finding one.
  • Compare like with like. Each county has its own models, so compare places within a county, and dates within a county.
  • Use the classes to prioritise. "High" and "Very high" areas are where scouting is most worthwhile.
  • Read forecasts as tendencies. A forecast says where conditions are expected to be more or less favourable than usual; check the nowcast as the month arrives.

When maps are updated

  • Weather downloads: every day.
  • Nowcast: every week. The map date is the latest day for which all observed weather is available, usually about a week ago.
  • Forecast: every month, a few days after the seasonal forecast is released on the 5th. A new forecast replaces the previous one for the months they share. Each month's map shows the issue date in the Month list.

Limits

  • The models only know the conditions of the field records they were trained on. Months or places with few records are less reliable.
  • Weather is much coarser than 30 m; temperature and humidity are adjusted for elevation, rainfall and wind are not.
  • Seasonal forecasts have limited skill beyond the first one or two months.
  • The forecast needs the satellite composites of the previous month. If they arrive late, the forecast is late too.

What runs on its own

Nowcastweekly, {{ nowcast_day }} {{ schedule.nowcast.hour }}:00Maps the latest date with observed weather and publishes it, unless that date is already published.

Runs wait in a queue and run one at a time in the pestmodels container. If an automatic run fails, it is retried a few hours later, up to three times per period. If a run cannot start because something is missing (a key, a model, last month's composites), the Processes page says what it is waiting for, and the run starts once it is fixed.

What you can do

  • Watch runs, their steps and their log on Processes.
  • Retry a failed run: it continues from the step that failed.
  • Start a run for a chosen period: download weather for past dates, map past nowcast dates, re-run a forecast month, or retrain the pest models after uploading new incidence records.
  • Pause an automatic schedule, e.g. during maintenance.
  • Upload what no download produces on Input data: static layers once, trained models and incidence records. The satellite composites are made in Earth Engine every month; a composite can still be uploaded (e.g. the team's history, or a month Earth Engine could not make), and an uploaded file is never replaced by the automatic fetch.
  • Model layers appear under Layers like uploaded ones; they can be hidden or deleted there.

One-time setup

  1. Google Earth Engine: a service account registered for Earth Engine. Put its JSON key on the server and set PESTWATCH_GEE_KEY_FILE (the path inside the container) and PESTWATCH_GEE_PROJECT in .env.
  2. Copernicus CDS: set CDS_API_KEY (and CDS_API_URL) in .env, then log in to the CDS website once and accept the licences of derived-era5-land-daily-statistics, derived-era5-single-levels-daily-statistics and seasonal-original-single-levels.
  3. Upload, for each county: the DEM, soil and crop layers and the pest models. Upload the modelling team's monthly composites from 2020-01 to the last month the LSTM was trained on (its pixel climatology is computed from them); later months are made in Earth Engine.
  4. Optionally copy the existing weather archive into the data folder to avoid downloading years of history.
